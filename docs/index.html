01 / OVERVIEW

Knowing the meaning
is only half the story.

When an LLM translates an idiom word for word, is it missing the knowledge—or failing to use what it knows?

IdiomDIT separates these possibilities by evaluating Detection, Interpretation, and Translation together. It reveals a persistent “know-but-error” pattern: models recognize an idiom and explain its meaning, yet still translate it literally. We call this Literal Translation Bias (LTB).

Across six language pairs and five models, the dominant failure mode depends on the translation direction. Prompting helps, but does not eliminate the errors. Probing and ablation offer a further lesson: apparent hidden-state evidence needs controls for idiom identity and random perturbations.

Read the full abstract +

Large language models persistently produce literal translations of idiomatic expressions, but the source of this failure remains unclear. We introduce IdiomDIT, a diagnostic framework that jointly evaluates idiom Detection, idiom Interpretation, and idiomatic Translation, attributing each error to a specific source. Across six language pairs and five models spanning 4B to 70B, IdiomDIT shows that literal translation error (LTE) can persist even after successful detection and interpretation, a know-but-error pattern we term Literal Translation Bias (LTB). This pattern is direction-specific: LTB dominates some directions, while others show greater knowledge deficits, likely reflecting lower idiom exposure in pretraining. We then use linear probing to test whether LTB is already reflected in the model’s pre-generation hidden state, recovering per-language literal translation directions with high accuracy, and ablating this direction reduces LTE. However, once we ensure no idiom’s data leaks across training and test, and subtract out each idiom’s mean hidden state before probing, accuracy collapses, confirming the probe was largely detecting which idiom it is looking at rather than a genuine bias signal. A matched random direction also reduces LTE just as much as the identified one. We release these controls as a reusable protocol for testing whether such hidden-state attribution claims in machine translation are genuine or confounded.

Same error. Different origins.

Literal translation bias and knowledge deficits contribute differently across directions.

01

The direction matters.

En→Fa, Fr→En, and Ko→En are dominated by LTB. Fa→En, Fi→En, and Ja→En show a mix of LTB and knowledge deficits.

02

Target the actual error.

Instructions to avoid literal translation reduce LTE more than generic meaning-preservation instructions in all six directions for Qwen3.5-9B.

03

Prompting has limits.

Even the strongest tested prompt leaves residual LTE, including 13.0% for French→English and 29.4% for Finnish→English.

04 / LOOKING INSIDE THE MODEL

A strong probe.
A necessary reality check.

Can hidden states reveal a direction that causes literal translation bias?

At first, linear probes distinguish “know-but-error” from “know-and-correct” cases with high accuracy. But the same idiom can appear in both training and validation data.

Separating idioms across folds sharply reduces performance. After removing idiom identity, the probe never exceeds a within-idiom permutation baseline in the five computable language pairs.

What the evidence supports

These experiments do not establish a bias-specific causal direction. Smaller samples for Finnish→English and Korean→English warrant additional caution; detailed paired ablations and robustness checks are in the paper.
